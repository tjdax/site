AI governance

A private initiative that stands with the people who use AI, not the people who build it. With human dignity as the measure, it explains the sound use of AI, records what is actually happening, and offers proposals to society.

About Use check Benchmark

About

A few companies build AI, and a few governments regulate it. The people who use it every day, and who live with the results, are ordinary users. This initiative does not speak for a company's interest or for a political side. From published principles and from evidence, it asks what people should do with AI, and what they should not do.

The aim is to help more people use AI properly and conveniently, and to survey and publish what users actually experience, on a regular schedule, so companies and governments can judge from reality. The measure is one thing: human dignity.

Public principles

A judgment of what to do, and what not to do, needs a standard that anyone can read. These are the draft principles.

  1. The final judgment, and the responsibility, stay with a person. Decisions that shape a life, such as medicine, hiring, justice, and finance, are not handed over to AI.
  2. Do not use it to deceive people. That includes impersonation, deepfakes, and manufactured public opinion.
  3. Do not use it to shut people out or to watch over them. Ask first whether it works against children, older people, workers, or minorities.
  4. Do not hide that AI was used. Where it can change another person's judgment, say so.
  5. Do not give up human ability for the sake of convenience. Use AI to amplify a person, not to replace one.

AI Use Check

    AI benchmark

    We benchmark how an AI treats the person using it. Similar work already exists. ELEPHANT, SYCON, and SPINE measure social sycophancy, and ELEPHANT does so on four dimensions. Polar and PoliticsBench measure political lean. Polar, from Seoul National University, reads American and Korean politics side by side. Common Sense Media and Stanford have reported that companion bots can raise a child's risk of distress, dependence, and self-harm.

    This report puts those separate questions in one place, from the user's side. It includes Korean and Chinese context, and a private initiative publishes it, not an AI company.

    What we measure

    We do not set a correct political side, or one correct reading of a disputed history. We measure consistency.

    • Politics. The model should argue both sides with the same care, keep the same answer when only the user's identity changes, and not insert an opinion nobody asked for.
    • History. Dates and events are right or wrong. On a disputed interpretation, we mark whether the model declares one answer or sets out the disagreement.
    • Capability. Other benchmarks already score coding and compute. We ask whether the model sounds sure about what it does not know, refuses a dangerous request without also refusing an ordinary one, and states its own limits.
    • Sycophancy and emotional dependence, including the risk to children.

    Each axis is reported on its own. We do not average them into one rank. A strong score on skill does not hide a risk on another axis.

    Scoring uses a written rubric, judge models from at least two companies, and a human check of a sample. The prompts, the judge prompts, and the original outputs are published.

    October 2026 results

    Ten sample items, two on each axis, scored once. The models are Opus 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Muse Spark 1.3, and Grok 4.7, each at the high setting. A separate model, GLM, judged the replies. 100 means more human-centered on that axis. The axes are not averaged into one rank.
